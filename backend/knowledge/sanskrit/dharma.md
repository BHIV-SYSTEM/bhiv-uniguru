# Dharma

## Canonical Name

Dharma

## Sanskrit

धर्म

## Transliteration

Dharma

## Śabda

धर्म

## Dhātu

धृ (Dhṛ) — To uphold, sustain, support.

## Vyākaraṇa

Derived from the verbal root धृ (dhṛ) with the suffix -man. Masculine noun, second declension. Pāṇini Ashtadhyayi 3.3.18 governs the suffix formation.

## Nirukta

Yāska's Nirukta: "dharayati iti dharmaḥ" — that which upholds is Dharma. The root dhṛ carries the sense of bearing, sustaining, maintaining cosmic and social order.

## Bīja

ध (Dha) — the seed syllable associated with sustaining force in Tantric traditions.

## Tattva

Ṛta-Dharma Tattva — the principle of cosmic and moral order that sustains all existence. In Sāṃkhya: Dharma is a quality (guṇa) of the Mahat (cosmic intellect). In Vedānta: Dharma is the expression of Ṛta at the human level.

## Śakti

Dharma-Śakti — the preserving and ordering power. Associated with Viṣṇu's sustaining function (sthiti-śakti). In Śākta traditions, Dharma is upheld by the Goddess as Mahāmāyā maintaining cosmic law.

## Literal Meaning

That which is upheld; that which upholds. The sustaining principle.

## Functional Meaning

Dharma is the universal principle governing righteous conduct, cosmic order, social duty, and individual ethics. It operates simultaneously at four levels: Ṛta (cosmic order), Varṇāśrama-dharma (social order), Svadharma (individual duty), and Sādhāraṇa-dharma (universal ethics applicable to all beings).

## Ontology

In Vaiśeṣika: Dharma is a padārtha (category of reality) — the cause of abhyudaya (worldly prosperity) and niḥśreyasa (liberation). In Mīmāṃsā: Dharma is codanā-lakṣaṇa — that which is enjoined by Vedic injunction. In Advaita Vedānta: Dharma at the vyāvahārika (conventional) level is real and necessary; at the pāramārthika (absolute) level, only Brahman exists.

## Cosmology

Dharma is one of the four Puruṣārthas (aims of human life): Dharma, Artha, Kāma, Mokṣa. In the Purāṇic cosmology, Dharma is personified as a deity, son of Brahmā, husband of ten daughters of Dakṣa. In the four yugas (Kṛta, Tretā, Dvāpara, Kali), Dharma stands on four legs, three, two, and one respectively — indicating progressive decline of cosmic order.

## Psychology

In Yoga psychology (Patañjali): Dharma-megha-samādhi is the highest samādhi, the "cloud of virtue" that rains liberation. Dharma as psychological principle governs the sattva-rajas-tamas balance in the individual. Right action (dharmic action) produces psychological clarity (citta-prasāda).

## Governance

Rājadharma (royal duty) is elaborated in Manusmṛti, Arthaśāstra, Mahābhārata Śāntiparva. The king's primary duty is dharma-rakṣaṇa (protection of dharma). Kauṭilya's Arthaśāstra treats dharma as the foundation of statecraft. Aśoka's dhamma edicts represent the historical application of dharmic governance.

## Medicine

In Āyurveda: Dharma is the foundation of Āyurvedic ethics. The physician's svadharma includes ahiṃsā (non-harm), satya (truth), and service. Caraka Saṃhitā Sūtrasthāna 1.15 states that the physician who follows dharma achieves both worldly success and liberation.

## Mathematics

Dharma as cosmic order underlies the mathematical precision of Vedic ritual geometry (Śulbasūtras). The Pythagorean theorem appears in Baudhāyana Śulbasūtra as a dharmic requirement for altar construction. Mathematical truth (satya) is considered a form of dharma.

## Astronomy

Ṛta (cosmic order, precursor to dharma) governs the movements of celestial bodies. Vedic astronomy (Jyotiṣa) treats planetary motion as dharmic — planets follow their ordained paths. The concept of ṛtu (season) derives from ṛta, linking dharma to astronomical cycles.

## Metallurgy

In Viśvakarmā tradition: the dharma of metals is their inherent nature and proper use. Gold (suvarṇa) has the dharma of purity; iron (ayasa) has the dharma of strength. Proper metallurgical practice follows the dharma of each material.

## Ritual

Yajña (sacrifice) is the primary ritual expression of dharma. The Mīmāṃsā school holds that Vedic ritual injunctions (vidhi) define dharma. Dharma-śāstra prescribes the sixteen saṃskāras (life-cycle rituals) as the ritual framework of dharmic life. Sandhyāvandana (daily worship) is the minimal dharmic ritual obligation.

## Symbolism

Dharma is symbolized by the wheel (dharmacakra) — the Buddha's first teaching is the "turning of the wheel of dharma." The scales of justice represent dharma's balancing function. The lotus represents dharma's purity amid worldly existence. The cow (go) is the living symbol of dharma in Vedic tradition.

## Traditional Interpretations

- Advaita Vedānta (Śaṅkara): Dharma is ultimately subsumed in jñāna (knowledge); it is a means, not the end
- Viśiṣṭādvaita (Rāmānuja): Dharma is bhakti-yoga — devotional service to Viṣṇu is the highest dharma
- Dvaita (Madhva): Dharma is the eternal will of Viṣṇu; individual dharma is participation in divine order
- Mīmāṃsā (Jaimini): Dharma is exclusively Vedic injunction; no dharma exists outside Veda
- Buddhism: Dharma is the teaching of the Buddha and the nature of reality (dhamma)
- Jainism: Dharma is the medium of motion (one of the six dravyas); also righteous conduct (dharma)

## Historical Evolution

- Ṛgvedic period: Ṛta (cosmic order) is the precursor; dharma appears rarely
- Atharvaveda: Dharma begins to acquire social and ethical dimensions
- Upaniṣadic period: Dharma becomes internalized as ethical-spiritual principle
- Epic period (Mahābhārata): Dharma becomes the central problematic — the Gītā's entire teaching is dharmic
- Dharmaśāstra period (200 BCE–1200 CE): Systematic codification of dharma in law texts
- Colonial period: Dharma reinterpreted through encounter with Western legal concepts
- Modern: Dharma as "religion" (mistranslation) vs. dharma as cosmic-ethical principle (ongoing debate)
