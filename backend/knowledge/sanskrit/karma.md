# Karma

## Canonical Name

Karma

## Sanskrit

कर्म

## Transliteration

Karma

## Śabda

कर्म

## Dhātu

कृ (Kṛ) — To do, perform, act, make.

## Vyākaraṇa

Derived from the verbal root कृ (kṛ) with the suffix -man (neuter noun). Pāṇini Ashtadhyayi 3.3.18. Karma is a neuter noun meaning "action" or "deed." In grammar, karma also denotes the object of a verb (karman = object/patient).

## Nirukta

Yāska: "kriyate anena iti karma" — that by which action is performed. The root kṛ encompasses all forms of doing, making, and performing.

## Bīja

क (Ka) — the seed syllable of action and creation in Tantric traditions. Associated with Brahmā (the creator) whose bīja is also Ka.

## Tattva

Karma-Tattva — the principle of causation through action. In Sāṃkhya: karma is the activity of the three guṇas (sattva, rajas, tamas). In Vaiśeṣika: karma is one of the seven padārthas (categories), defined as motion. In Vedānta: karma is the mechanism of saṃsāra (cycle of rebirth).

## Śakti

Kriyā-Śakti — the power of action. One of the three fundamental śaktis (icchā-jñāna-kriyā). Karma operates through kriyā-śakti as the dynamic force of manifestation.

## Literal Meaning

Action; deed; work; ritual act.

## Functional Meaning

Karma is the universal principle that every action generates corresponding consequences. It operates across three dimensions: Sañcita karma (accumulated past actions), Prārabdha karma (karma currently bearing fruit), and Āgāmi/Kriyamāṇa karma (karma being created now). The law of karma is not punishment but the natural law of cause and effect operating through consciousness.

## Ontology

In Mīmāṃsā: karma (ritual action) is the primary means of achieving dharmic results; the apūrva (unseen potency) of ritual karma persists until it bears fruit. In Advaita Vedānta: karma operates only at the vyāvahārika level; from the absolute standpoint, there is no doer, hence no karma. In Viśiṣṭādvaita: karma is real and must be exhausted through bhakti and surrender to Viṣṇu. In Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika: karma is one of seven categories of reality, defined as motion (pariṇāma).

## Cosmology

Karma drives the cycle of saṃsāra across all fourteen lokas. The Purāṇas describe karma as the mechanism by which souls transmigrate through different realms. In Jain cosmology, karma is a subtle material substance (karma-pudgala) that adheres to the soul (jīva) and determines its condition. The Bhāgavata Purāṇa describes karma as the force that maintains the cosmic order of creation, preservation, and dissolution.

## Psychology

In Yoga psychology: karma creates saṃskāras (mental impressions) and vāsanās (latent tendencies) that condition future experience. Patañjali's Yoga Sūtras 2.12-14 describe the karma-āśaya (karma-store) as the root of birth, lifespan, and experience. Karma operates through the kāraṇa-śarīra (causal body). Karma yoga (the yoga of action) transforms karma from bondage to liberation by eliminating the sense of doership (ahaṃkāra).

## Governance

Karma as social principle: the concept of karma-phala (fruit of action) underlies Indian jurisprudence. Kauṭilya's Arthaśāstra recognizes that rulers accumulate karma through their governance. The concept of rāja-dharma (royal duty) is the karmic responsibility of the king. Historical Indian governance was explicitly framed in karmic terms — Aśoka's dhamma edicts reflect karmic accountability.

## Medicine

In Āyurveda: karma is one of the causes of disease (prājñāparādha — crime against wisdom is a karmic cause). Caraka Saṃhitā identifies karma as a factor in the etiology of disease. Karmic diseases (ādhidaivika) require spiritual remedies alongside physical treatment. The concept of karma-ja-vyādhi (karma-born disease) is a distinct category in Āyurvedic pathology.

## Mathematics

The mathematical principle of karma is the law of conservation: every action has an equal and opposite reaction. Vedic mathematics (Gaṇita) treats karma as the principle of balance — equations must balance, debts must be settled. The concept of ṛṇa (debt) in Vedic mathematics mirrors the karmic debt concept.

## Astronomy

Karma in Jyotiṣa (Vedic astrology): the birth chart (janma-kuṇḍali) is considered a map of prārabdha karma. Planetary positions at birth reflect accumulated karma. The concept of daśā (planetary period) describes the timing of karmic fruition. Graha (planets) are considered agents of karma delivery.

## Metallurgy

In Viśvakarmā tradition: the karma of the craftsman (śilpin) determines the quality of the work. The concept of karma-yoga in craft: the craftsman who works without attachment to results produces the finest work. The dharma of metals (their inherent properties) must be respected — working against a metal's karma produces inferior results.

## Ritual

Karma-kāṇḍa (ritual section of the Vedas) is the primary domain of Mīmāṃsā. The sixteen saṃskāras are karmic rituals that shape the individual's karmic trajectory. Yajña (sacrifice) is the highest karmic act — it purifies accumulated karma and generates positive karma. Prāyaścitta (expiation) rituals neutralize specific karmic debts.

## Symbolism

The wheel (cakra) symbolizes karma's cyclical nature. The scales represent karmic balance. The seed (bīja) represents karma's potential — every action is a seed that will bear fruit. The mirror represents karma's reflective nature — what you do returns to you. The knot (granthi) represents karmic bondage.

## Traditional Interpretations

- Advaita Vedānta (Śaṅkara): Karma is ultimately unreal from the absolute standpoint; jñāna (knowledge) alone destroys karma
- Viśiṣṭādvaita (Rāmānuja): Karma is real and must be surrendered to Viṣṇu through prapatti (surrender)
- Dvaita (Madhva): Karma is eternally real; liberation requires Viṣṇu's grace to transcend karma
- Mīmāṃsā: Karma (ritual action) is the primary means of achieving all human goals
- Buddhism: Karma is intentional action (cetanā); it is the intention, not the act, that creates karma
- Jainism: Karma is a subtle material substance that binds the soul; liberation requires exhausting all karma through tapas (austerity)

## Historical Evolution

- Ṛgvedic period: Karma primarily means ritual action; cosmic karma (ṛta) governs the universe
- Brāhmaṇa period: Karma as ritual action is elaborated extensively; karma-kāṇḍa develops
- Upaniṣadic period: Karma as ethical-spiritual principle emerges; karma-saṃsāra connection established
- Buddhist period (500 BCE): Karma redefined as intentional action; karma-rebirth doctrine systematized
- Jain period: Karma as material substance — unique and influential formulation
- Epic-Purāṇic period: Karma as cosmic justice mechanism; karma-yoga as path to liberation
- Medieval: Karma integrated into bhakti traditions — karma surrendered to God
