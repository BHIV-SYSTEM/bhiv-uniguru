# Moksha

## Canonical Name

Moksha

## Sanskrit

मोक्ष

## Transliteration

Mokṣa

## Śabda

मोक्ष

## Dhātu

मुच् (Muc) — To release, to free, to liberate.

## Vyākaraṇa

Mokṣa is a masculine noun derived from the root muc (to release) with the suffix -a. Related forms: mukti (liberation), vimokṣa (complete liberation), apavarga (Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika term for liberation).

## Nirukta

"Mucyate iti mokṣaḥ" — that from which one is released, or the state of being released. Liberation from the cycle of birth and death (saṃsāra) and from the bondage of ignorance (avidyā).

## Bīja

ह्रीं (Hrīm) — the bīja of liberation in Śākta traditions. Also ॐ (Om) as the sound of the liberated state.

## Tattva

Mokṣa-Tattva — the principle of ultimate liberation. The fourth and highest Puruṣārtha (aim of human life), transcending Dharma, Artha, and Kāma. In Advaita: Mokṣa is the recognition of what always already is — Ātman = Brahman. In Sāṃkhya: Mokṣa is the Puruṣa's recognition of its eternal distinction from Prakṛti (kaivalya).

## Śakti

Mukti-Śakti — the liberating power. In Śākta traditions, the Goddess grants mokṣa through her grace (anugraha-śakti). In Vaiṣṇava traditions, Viṣṇu's grace (prasāda) is the liberating power.

## Literal Meaning

Liberation; release; freedom from bondage.

## Functional Meaning

Mokṣa is freedom from the cycle of birth, death, and rebirth (saṃsāra) and from the root cause of that cycle — ignorance (avidyā) and its product, ego (ahaṃkāra). It is not a place or a future state but the recognition of one's true nature as already free. The Upaniṣads teach that bondage and liberation are both appearances — the Ātman was never bound. Different schools describe mokṣa differently: as jīvanmukti (liberation while living), videhamukti (liberation at death), sāyujya (union with God), sālokya (dwelling in God's realm), sārūpya (having God's form), sāmīpya (proximity to God).

## Ontology

In Advaita Vedānta: Mokṣa is the removal of the superimposition (adhyāsa) of non-self on the Self. It is not an achievement but a recognition. Jīvanmukti (liberation while living) is possible. In Viśiṣṭādvaita: Mokṣa is eternal service to Viṣṇu in Vaikuṇṭha — the soul retains its individuality. In Dvaita: Mokṣa is eternal blissful service to Viṣṇu — the soul never merges with Brahman. In Sāṃkhya: Kaivalya (aloneness) — Puruṣa rests in its own nature, free from Prakṛti's entanglement. In Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika: Apavarga — the cessation of all pain and pleasure; a state of pure existence without consciousness (controversial).

## Cosmology

In Purāṇic cosmology: liberated souls (muktas) dwell in Brahmaloka (Satyaloka) until the final dissolution (mahāpralaya), after which they merge with Brahman. In Vaiṣṇava cosmology: liberated souls dwell eternally in Vaikuṇṭha (Viṣṇu's realm) or Goloka (Kṛṣṇa's realm). In Śaiva cosmology: liberated souls merge with Śiva or dwell in Śivaloka. The concept of krama-mukti (gradual liberation) describes the soul's journey through higher realms before final liberation.

## Psychology

Mokṣa as psychological state: the liberated person (jīvanmukta) lives in the world but is not bound by it. The Yoga Vāsiṣṭha describes the psychology of liberation in detail: the liberated mind is like a burnt rope — it retains its form but has no binding power. The characteristics of a jīvanmukta include: equanimity (samatvam), freedom from desire (vairāgya), compassion (karuṇā), and spontaneous right action (sahaja-karma).

## Governance

The concept of mokṣa in governance: the ideal ruler seeks mokṣa through dharmic governance. The Arthaśāstra acknowledges mokṣa as the ultimate goal even for kings. The concept of rāja-ṛṣi (royal sage) — a king who governs while pursuing liberation — is the Vedic ideal. Janaka (king of Videha) is the paradigmatic example of a ruler who achieved mokṣa while governing.

## Medicine

In Āyurveda: mokṣa is the ultimate health — freedom from all disease at the root level. The concept of mokṣa-dharma in Āyurveda: the physician's highest service is to guide patients toward liberation. The Caraka Saṃhitā states that the ultimate aim of Āyurveda is not just physical health but mokṣa.

## Mathematics

The concept of mokṣa in mathematics: liberation from the cycle of saṃsāra mirrors the mathematical concept of escaping an infinite series. The concept of ananta (infinity) in Indian mathematics is connected to the infinite nature of the liberated state. The Upaniṣadic statement "pūrṇam adaḥ pūrṇam idam" (that is full, this is full) describes the mathematical completeness of the liberated state.

## Astronomy

In Jyotiṣa: the 12th house (vyaya bhāva) in the birth chart represents mokṣa. The nakṣatra Abhijit is associated with mokṣa. The concept of uttarāyaṇa (the sun's northward journey) is associated with the path of liberation (devayāna) in the Upaniṣads.

## Metallurgy

In alchemical traditions: mokṣa is the alchemical gold — the final transmutation of the base metal of ego into the pure gold of liberated consciousness. The Rasaśāstra tradition treats the alchemical process as a metaphor for spiritual liberation.

## Ritual

The Mahāmṛtyuñjaya Mantra is the primary ritual for liberation from the death-cycle. The Śrāddha ritual is performed to assist the departed soul on its journey toward mokṣa. The Kāśī (Varanasi) pilgrimage is considered a direct path to mokṣa — dying in Kāśī is believed to grant liberation. The Gāyatrī Mantra is considered a mokṣa-mantra when understood at its deepest level.

## Symbolism

The open sky (ākāśa) symbolizes mokṣa — boundless, free, uncontained. The bird in flight symbolizes the liberated soul. The lotus rising from mud symbolizes mokṣa — purity emerging from worldly existence. The extinguished flame (nirvāṇa) symbolizes mokṣa in Buddhist tradition. The open door symbolizes mokṣa — the passage from bondage to freedom.

## Related Deities

- Viṣṇu (the liberator — grants mokṣa through grace)
- Śiva (Maheśvara — the great lord who grants liberation)
- Devī (Tārā — the Goddess who carries across the ocean of saṃsāra)
- Yama (Dharmarāja — the lord who oversees the transition to liberation)
- Gaṇeśa (the remover of obstacles on the path to mokṣa)

## Related Lokas

- Brahmaloka (Satyaloka) — the realm of liberation
- Vaikuṇṭha (Viṣṇu's realm — Vaiṣṇava mokṣa)
- Kailāsa (Śiva's realm — Śaiva mokṣa)
- Goloka (Kṛṣṇa's realm — highest Vaiṣṇava mokṣa)

## Related Koshas

- Beyond all five koshas is the liberated Ātman
- The dissolution of identification with all koshas is mokṣa
- Ānandamaya Kosha (bliss sheath) is the last veil before mokṣa

## Related Chakras

- Sahasrāra (crown chakra) — the chakra of liberation; when kuṇḍalinī reaches here, mokṣa is attained
- Brahmarandhra (the aperture at the crown) — the exit point of the liberated soul

## Related Yantras

## Related Vidyās

- Brahmavidyā (knowledge of Brahman = mokṣa)

- Mokṣa-Dharma Vidyā (the science of liberation — Mahābhārata Śāntiparva)

## Related Śāstras

- Upaniṣads (primary source)
- Bhagavad Gītā (the entire text is a teaching on mokṣa)
- Brahma Sūtras
- Yoga Sūtras (kaivalya = mokṣa)
- Yoga Vāsiṣṭha (psychology of liberation)
- Vivekacūḍāmaṇi (Śaṅkara)

## Traditional Interpretations

- Advaita Vedānta: Mokṣa is the recognition of Ātman = Brahman; jīvanmukti is possible
- Viśiṣṭādvaita: Mokṣa is eternal service to Viṣṇu in Vaikuṇṭha; soul retains individuality
- Dvaita: Mokṣa is eternal blissful service to Viṣṇu; soul never merges with Brahman
- Sāṃkhya: Kaivalya — Puruṣa rests in its own nature, free from Prakṛti
- Buddhism: Nirvāṇa — the extinguishing of craving, aversion, and delusion
- Jainism: Mokṣa — the soul (jīva) freed from all karma, dwelling at the top of the universe (Siddhashila)

## Historical Evolution

- Ṛgvedic period: No explicit mokṣa concept; immortality (amṛtatva) is the goal
- Upaniṣadic period: Mokṣa as liberation from saṃsāra established
- Buddhist period: Nirvāṇa as the Buddhist equivalent of mokṣa
- Jain period: Mokṣa as the soul's liberation from karma-matter
- Epic-Purāṇic period: Mokṣa as the fourth Puruṣārtha; various paths described
- Medieval bhakti: Mokṣa through devotion (bhakti) — accessible to all
- Modern: Mokṣa reinterpreted as psychological liberation, social liberation, ecological liberation
