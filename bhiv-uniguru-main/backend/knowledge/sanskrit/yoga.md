# Yoga

## Canonical Name

Yoga

## Sanskrit

योग

## Transliteration

Yoga

## Śabda

योग

## Dhātu

युज् (Yuj) — To yoke, to unite, to join, to concentrate.

## Vyākaraṇa

Yoga is a masculine noun derived from the root yuj with the suffix -a. The root yuj has two meanings: (1) yujir yoge — to unite; (2) yuj samādhau — to concentrate. Both meanings are operative in the concept of yoga.

## Nirukta

"Yujyate anena iti yogaḥ" — that by which union is achieved. Also: "yoge yukto bhavet" — one who is yoked (disciplined) is in yoga.

## Bīja

यं (Yaṃ) — the bīja of the air element (vāyu), associated with the heart chakra (Anāhata) and the breath-based practices of yoga.

## Tattva

Yoga-Tattva — the principle of union and integration. Yoga operates at multiple levels: union of individual self with universal Self (Advaita), union of Puruṣa with Prakṛti's dissolution (Sāṃkhya-Yoga), union of devotee with God (Bhakti-Yoga), union of action with non-attachment (Karma-Yoga).

## Śakti

Yoga-Śakti — the integrating power. The kuṇḍalinī-śakti is the primary yogic power that, when awakened, unites the individual with the universal. Prāṇa-śakti (life-force) is the medium through which yoga operates.

## Literal Meaning

Union; yoking; discipline; method; path.

## Functional Meaning

Yoga is the discipline leading to union of the individual self with the highest reality. Patañjali defines yoga as "yogaś citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ" — yoga is the cessation of the modifications of the mind-stuff. The Bhagavad Gītā defines yoga as "samatvam yoga ucyate" — equanimity is called yoga, and "yogaḥ karmasu kauśalam" — yoga is skill in action. The four classical paths are: Jñāna-Yoga (knowledge), Bhakti-Yoga (devotion), Karma-Yoga (action), Rāja-Yoga (meditation).

## Ontology

In Sāṃkhya-Yoga: yoga is the practical discipline that achieves the Sāṃkhya goal of Puruṣa's liberation from Prakṛti. In Advaita Vedānta: yoga is a means (sādhana) to the recognition of Ātman = Brahman. In Viśiṣṭādvaita: Bhakti-Yoga is the highest yoga — devotional union with Viṣṇu. In Tantra: yoga is the union of Śiva and Śakti within the practitioner's body.

## Cosmology

In Purāṇic cosmology: yoga is the cosmic principle of integration that holds the universe together. The concept of Yoga-Māyā (Viṣṇu's creative yoga-power) describes the divine yoga that maintains cosmic order. The Purāṇas describe Viṣṇu as eternally in yoga (yoga-nidrā — the yogic sleep of Viṣṇu).

## Psychology

Patañjali's Yoga Sūtras provide the most systematic psychology of yoga. The eight limbs (aṣṭāṅga): Yama (restraints), Niyama (observances), Āsana (posture), Prāṇāyāma (breath control), Pratyāhāra (sense withdrawal), Dhāraṇā (concentration), Dhyāna (meditation), Samādhi (absorption). The psychology of yoga maps the transformation of consciousness from ordinary awareness to liberation. The concept of citta (mind-stuff) and its five states (kṣipta, mūḍha, vikṣipta, ekāgra, niruddha) describes the psychological journey of yoga.

## Governance

The concept of yoga in governance: the Arthaśāstra recommends yogic practices for the ruler's mental clarity and decision-making. The concept of rāja-yoga (royal yoga) is both a spiritual path and a metaphor for the disciplined governance of the kingdom of the self. Aśoka's dhamma practice was essentially a form of governance-yoga.

## Medicine

Yoga as medicine: the Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā describes yoga as the cure for all diseases. Modern research confirms yoga's efficacy for stress, cardiovascular disease, mental health, and chronic pain. In Āyurveda: yoga is one of the three pillars of health alongside āhāra (diet) and vihāra (lifestyle). The concept of yoga-cikitsā (yoga therapy) is a distinct branch of Āyurvedic medicine.

## Mathematics

The concept of yoga in mathematics: the Vedic mathematical concept of "yoga" (addition/union) mirrors the spiritual concept. The Sulbasūtras use geometric yoga (union of areas) to construct ritual altars. The concept of saṃyoga (conjunction) in Vaiśeṣika philosophy is the mathematical-ontological basis of yoga.

## Astronomy

In Jyotiṣa: yoga refers to specific planetary combinations (graha-yoga) that indicate particular life patterns. There are 27 yogas in the Vedic calendar (based on the combined longitude of Sun and Moon). The concept of yoga in Jyotiṣa is directly derived from the root meaning of "union" — the union of planetary influences.

## Metallurgy

In alchemical traditions: yoga is the process of combining (yojana) substances to create new compounds. The Rasaśāstra tradition uses the concept of yoga (combination) as the basis of alchemical practice. The yoga of mercury (pārada) with sulfur (gandhaka) is the foundational alchemical yoga.

## Ritual

The Yoga-Sūtra's concept of Īśvara-praṇidhāna (surrender to God) is the ritual dimension of yoga. The concept of pūjā (worship) as yoga: the Bhagavad Gītā describes devotional worship as Bhakti-Yoga. The Tantric ritual of nyāsa (placing mantras on the body) is a form of yoga that unites the practitioner with the deity.

## Symbolism

The yoke (yoga) symbolizes the disciplined harnessing of the mind. The lotus in meditation posture symbolizes yoga — rooted in the earth, blooming toward the sky. The intertwined serpents (kuṇḍalinī) symbolize the yogic energy. The bindu (point of concentration) symbolizes the goal of yoga — one-pointed awareness.

## Traditional Interpretations

- Patañjali: Yoga is the cessation of mental modifications; the goal is kaivalya (liberation)
- Advaita Vedānta: Yoga is a means to the recognition of Ātman = Brahman
- Viśiṣṭādvaita: Bhakti-Yoga is the highest yoga — devotional union with Viṣṇu
- Tantra: Yoga is the union of Śiva and Śakti within the practitioner's body
- Buddhism: Yoga (dhyāna) is the path to nirvāṇa
- Modern: Yoga as physical-mental wellness practice (partial understanding of the full tradition)

## Historical Evolution

- Ṛgvedic period: Yoga as yoking (of horses, of the mind to the divine)
- Upaniṣadic period: Yoga as the path to Brahman-realization
- Pre-classical period: Various yoga traditions develop (Jñāna, Bhakti, Karma)
- Classical period (Patañjali, ~200 BCE–200 CE): Yoga systematized in eight limbs
- Tantric period (500–1200 CE): Haṭha-Yoga, Kuṇḍalinī-Yoga develop
- Medieval bhakti: Bhakti-Yoga as the primary path for the common person
- Modern (19th century onward): Yoga globalized; physical aspects emphasized
